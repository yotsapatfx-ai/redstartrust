# CFTC Opens Comment on Compute Derivatives as Its Chairman Ties Them to the AI Race

Rules come after questions.

The **U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC)** has opened comment on derivatives contracts written on computing power, and its chairman put the stakes plainly: America cannot win the AI race without a robust derivatives market for compute.

## What the CFTC did

The agency issued a **request for comment** under **release number 9286-26**, dated **19 August 2026** and datelined Washington, on the listing of compute derivatives contracts. Its stated purpose is to improve its own **understanding and oversight of derivatives markets in compute**.

Two terms are worth unpacking. A derivative is a contract whose value comes not from itself but from something else it references. A request for comment is the stage at which an agency collects information and views from the people involved, before it decides whether to write any rules at all.

Here the thing being referenced is compute, in the CFTC's own framing.

## The chairman's reasoning

**Chairman Michael S. Selig** said that **"America cannot win the AI race without a robust derivatives market for compute."**

Chairman Selig added that American markets would establish the standards for compute trading the way they once did for industrial commodities, and he called the request **"the first step toward establishing clear rules."**

## The five things the agency wants to know

The questions a regulator chooses to ask tend to reveal what it is worried about.

The CFTC is seeking views on **compute cash market size and liquidity** — how big the underlying market actually is and how easily it trades. It is also asking about **market oversight considerations**, about **manipulation concerns**, and about **customer protection issues**. The fifth topic is **perpetual compute futures**, which the agency lists for comment without elaborating; the phrase describes a futures contract with no expiry date.

Beyond those five, the request invites feedback on **all aspects of compute markets**.

## Why this is only a first step

Nothing about this creates an obligation. A request for comment sets no requirement, bars no conduct, and does not commit the agency to a rule at the end of it. What it does is gather information from market participants so the regulator can see the shape of the market before deciding anything. Chairman Selig used the same framing himself when he described the exercise as a first step.

## The clock and the paperwork

The comment clock does not start with the announcement. It runs from the day the notice appears in the **Federal Register**, the US government's official journal of record. Comments are due **60 days after publication in the Federal Register**, and the release does not give that publication date. The related citation is **91 FR 54259**.

A regulator that has to begin by asking how large a market is, and how liquid, is saying something about how far ahead of the rulebook that market has already run.
